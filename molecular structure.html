4.1

The Kössel–Lewis approach

Atoms bond because isolated atoms (except noble gases) are not energetically stable. Kössel and Lewis reasoned that atoms react to complete their outer shell to 8 electrons — the octet rule — either by transferring electrons (ionic bond) or sharing them (covalent bond).

Pick an element to see its valence electrons and how many more it needs (or loses) to reach an octet.

Octet rule limitations: it fails for molecules with an odd number of electrons (NO), for electron-deficient species (BF3, has only 6 around B), and for expanded octets (SF6, PCl5), where the central atom uses d-orbitals to hold more than 8 electrons.
4.2

Ionic (electrovalent) bonding

Formed by complete transfer of electrons between atoms of very different electronegativity, producing oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction.

Formation favoured by

Low ionization enthalpyof the metal
High electron gain enthalpyof the non-metal
High lattice enthalpyof the compound formed

Lattice enthalpy

Energy released when gaseous ions come together to form 1 mole of a solid ionic crystal. It rises with higher ionic charge and falls with larger ionic radius — which is why MgO (charges 2+/2−, small ions) has a far higher lattice enthalpy than NaCl.

4.4

VSEPR theory — predicting shape

Electron pairs around a central atom — bonding or lone — repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs (lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp), which is why lone pairs squeeze bond angles inward.

4.5

Valence bond theory

VBT treats a covalent bond as forming when two half-filled atomic orbitals on different atoms overlap. Greater overlap gives a stronger bond. Overlap along the internuclear axis produces a sigma (σ) bond; sideways overlap of parallel p-orbitals produces a pi (π) bond, always accompanying a σ bond in a double or triple bond.

s–s overlap

H₂: head-on overlap of two 1s orbitals — a pure σ bond.

s–p overlap

HF: H's 1s overlaps end-on with F's half-filled 2p — σ bond.

p–p overlap

Cl₂: end-on p–p gives σ; sideways p–p (as in O₂, N₂) gives π.

4.6

Hybridization

Atomic orbitals on the same atom mix to form new, equivalent hybrid orbitals before bonding — this is what actually gives molecules their observed, symmetric shapes. Pick a molecule to see its hybridization.

4.7 – 4.8

Molecular orbital theory & homonuclear diatomics

MOT fills molecular orbitals (formed from combining atomic orbitals) in order of energy, same as the aufbau principle for atoms. Bond order = ½(bonding electrons − antibonding electrons) — and it correctly predicts that O₂ is paramagnetic, something Lewis structures cannot explain.

4.9

Hydrogen bonding

A weak-to-moderate attraction between a hydrogen atom (bonded to a highly electronegative F, O or N) and a lone pair on an electronegative atom nearby — either in another molecule (intermolecular) or within the same molecule (intramolecular).

Intermolecular

Between separate molecules — e.g. HF···HF chains, or the network in H₂O that gives it an anomalously high boiling point. Raises melting/boiling point and viscosity.

Intramolecular

Within a single molecule — e.g. in ortho-nitrophenol. Often lowers boiling point relative to the intermolecular-bonded isomer, since the H is "used up" internally.
